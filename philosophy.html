Teaching Philosophy

Warm, practical, and story-led—grounded in what happens in the trenches of a public-school dance room, not in abstraction.

I believe every student deserves the chance to learn the universal language of dance. Ballet is my first love and the foundation of my technique classes, but dance in a public school is about far more than steps. It is where students build physical health, confidence, and the social skills to work with people who are different from them.

I hold every student to the same standards and change how I teach, not what I expect. I learn each student as an individual rather than through a diagnosis or a label. I build relationships that balance authority with mutual respect, because students who feel known stay engaged. And I hand leadership to students as often as I can: they create choreography together, reteach each other, and assess their own growth.

When students of every ability perform together on one stage, something happens that the audience can feel. That is what dance education can do, and it is why I teach.

Lessons learned as an educator

  1. Let students tell you what they need—no assumptions based on a label.
  2. Use your caseload managers, therapists, and especially paraprofessionals—and get them dancing.
  3. Adapt classroom supports from other subjects for dance.
  4. Don’t change the standard; change the instructional method.
  5. Teach the class together, and let it teach itself.

Frameworks in practice

Tiffany names frameworks only when she can show what they look like on a Tuesday in Dance 1: CASEL SEL competencies in improvisation and ensemble work; Gholdy Muhammad’s five pursuits (Identity, Skills, Intellect, Criticality, Joy); Liz Lerman’s Critical Response Process for peer feedback; and developmentally safe practice from dance kinesiology study.
