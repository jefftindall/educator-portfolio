Dance for Every Body

Dance for Every Body: Dance for All Abilities — NDEO National Conference, October 2025 (Disability and Pedagogy track).

Hero media: concert group photo or Eric clapping story clip (with student media release).

Inclusive Dance 1 at Woodland High School brings general and special education students together to learn the same standards and perform on the same stage each semester. The program grew from Karina’s Class, which began at Steps of Faith Dance Studio for a student who “desperately wanted to take dance too.” Tiffany taught that class for seven years; after the studio closed during the pandemic, she continued with principal approval at Woodland HS.

Three strategies

Accommodate the student, not the diagnosis

Meet the learner in front of you. Research informs practice; it does not replace watching and listening.

Leverage existing resources

Learning Ladders, OT collaboration, and coordinated goals with classroom teachers turn supports into shared language across the building.

Embrace social emotional learning

Belonging, regulation, and peer teaching are part of technique—not add-ons.

Dance One etiquette learning ladder

Respect the space

Stories from the studio and the stage

Names are first names only; some stories need family consent before video or photos are published.

Eric and clapping

Eric could not tolerate clapping because of injuries from a childhood car accident. Instead of removing him from class, the class moved from finger snaps to golf claps to a dance with clapping built in. By semester’s end he clapped onstage and attended professional performances.

John and the volume

Research suggests some students prefer low stimulation; John preferred bright lights and loud music. Tiffany waited and watched before changing anything—a reminder not to assume from a label.

Kyler and communication

A non-verbal five-year-old in Karina’s Class responded to basic ASL (Stop, Go, Dance, Sit, More, Done). He began making eye contact and seeking affection in class.

Molly and jumping

Molly could not jump at age eight. Ten years later she jumps two feet to two feet, crosses the floor with grand battements, and says jumping is her favorite part of class.

Cheyenne and collaboration

Cheyenne wanted to run every group. Five years of structured choreography projects taught her to contribute as a team member. She is now in Project SEARCH at Piedmont Cartersville, on a path to employment.

Alyssia and belonging

Worried she could not fully participate, Alyssia learned about Full Radius Dance and Heather Whitestone. She is now a senior and work-based learning assistant teacher in the program.

Benefits

  • Students with disabilities: access to standards, performance, and peer community on equal footing.
  • General education students: empathy, leadership, and collaboration with classmates they might not otherwise know.
  • Teachers and special education staff: shared vocabulary, interdisciplinary goals, and joy in the work.
